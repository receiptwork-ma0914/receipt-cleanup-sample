# Little Garden — educator and co-play guide

## Purpose and prerequisites

Offer playful opportunities to recognize circle, square and triangle; match familiar colours using additional visible cues; sort by one property; and connect one, two and three objects with number words and a matching collection. The intended starting point is approximately age three, with wide individual variation. No reading, typing, colour vision, prior number-symbol knowledge or spoken answer is required. A child can tap, point for an adult to tap, or watch first. An adult should be nearby and able to demonstrate a simple tap.

Try **one to three minutes per activity**, or less. The three activities can be entered in any order. There are no timers, numeric scores, streaks or lost lives. Exploration check marks record completed activity sequences, not mastery. Stopping early is always fine.

## Start together

1. On the welcome page, read the short grown-up note and choose **Grown-up: let’s play**.
2. Tap one of the three illustrated activity cards.
3. Point at the two pictures and arrow in the demonstration. A bundled spoken prompt explains the same action after this intentional tap. Let the child replay it or mute it.
4. Choose **My turn**. Offer a hint, demonstrate again or stop whenever the child needs.

The grown-up guide is available from every view. The exit button returns to the welcome page. The garden button returns to activity selection. “Start again” resets only the current activity; a full garden reset is in the guide and completed lesson summary. State is kept only in browser memory and clears on refresh.

## Shape beds

- Demonstration: point from one circle to its identical match.
- Guided practice: match a circle, a square and a triangle, each initially identical in size and orientation.
- Fresh application: the final square choices are smaller and turned. The target is unchanged. Explain that turning the paper does not change its shape.
- Wrong matches keep the same target and offer a specific property cue, such as a circle being round with no corners. The matching choice gets a dashed outline. There is unlimited retry.
- Adult language: “What do you notice?” or “Let’s trace the edge.” Avoid teaching a turned square as a different shape called a diamond.

**Adaptation:** choose **Two choices** in the grown-up guide. This removes one distractor while always keeping the target. Start with the circle and stop after that round if needed. An adult may describe or tactually trace a large real shape alongside the diagram. The default offers only three choices.

## Colour baskets

- Demonstration: select a red flower with dots, then its red dotted basket.
- Guided practice: tap one flower, then one of two matching baskets. The selected flower has a thick outline and highlighted backing. Successfully placed flowers appear in their basket.
- Fresh application: a second tray changes the order and group sizes. The groups remain the same.
- Red is always paired with dots, blue with stripes. **Only one classification is requested: the colour group.** Patterns are redundant labels for those same groups, not a second independent condition. All flowers have the same silhouette and size.
- Wrong baskets keep the flower selected and cue the matching pattern; no objects are lost.

**Adaptation:** show one flower at a time by pointing, or have the adult select it and let the child choose the basket. Name the colour and pattern together. If colour naming is not comfortable, use “dots with dots” and “stripes with stripes.” Make matching paper cues for use away from the screen.

## Growing together

- Demonstration: one flower here, one flower in the other garden, connected by an arrow.
- Guided practice: tap **Count a flower** to reveal each target once. The newest flower gets an outline and each revealed flower a check mark. Say the number word while pointing to that flower. The reveal action stops at the target count, so repeated taps cannot count the same object twice.
- After the target is visible, use **Grow** to add flowers to the other patch and **−** to remove a flower. Choose the check button when the groups match. Too few or too many flowers gets a specific add/remove suggestion and one-to-one picture hint.
- Groups progress from one to two to three. The third target is arranged in a small triangular cluster while the matching garden uses a row: a new arrangement does not change how many.
- The last number word names the total. Repeat the total after counting: “One, two, three. Three flowers.”

**Adaptation:** stay with the one-flower round; point to each reference flower while growing its partner. Let the child move large real blocks or point while an adult controls the screen. Do not require recognizing written numerals. Work slowly without a timer.

## Sound, movement and access

The main demonstration and activity prompts, one/two/three counting words and brief generic feedback are bundled recordings. Native browser speech optionally supplies more detailed dynamic feedback. Both are initiated by intentional actions; mute is always available. If audio cannot play, the lesson still works through pictures, matching patterns, outlines and an adult's demonstration. Replay is available throughout an activity.

All interaction uses native buttons, with at least 64 CSS pixels in both dimensions. Keyboard users can use Tab/Shift+Tab and Enter/Space, with visible focus. There is no dragging, required animation, flashing, autoplay audio or time limit. Reduced-motion settings are respected. Exact browser checks and limitations are in TEST_REPORT.md.

## References and design rationale

1. **Linda M. Platas, DREME Family Math / Stanford: “All About Counting.”**
   https://familymath.stanford.edu/for-educators/counting-all-about/
   Accessed September 15, 2026 UTC. Supports using one number word for each object, understanding that the last number word gives the total, and recognizing that counting order does not change the amount. The reveal sequence, final total prompt and matching collection apply those principles. This is guidance for design, not validation of this app.

2. **Linda Dauksas and Jeanne White, NAEYC: “Discovering Shapes and Space in Preschool.”**
   https://www.naeyc.org/node/658
   Accessed September 15, 2026 UTC. Supports exploring and comparing familiar shapes in varied sizes and orientations through adult-supported play. The identical matches followed by a smaller, turned square apply that idea. This does not imply that every three-year-old should complete each round.

## Model limits and responsible use

The app checks a few visible matches against fixed examples. It cannot infer a child's reasoning, developmental stage, language understanding, vision or motor skills. Success may reflect imitation or an adult's help; mistakes may reflect unfamiliar controls. There is no diagnosis, validated learning-gain claim, adaptive assessment or universal age-suitability claim. This original submission was developed with simulated learner journeys and agent-operated browser review, not tests with children or a human-user study. No child data is requested or collected.

## Offline follow-up

With an adult, use **large** paper shapes and large blocks. Match a circle, square and triangle; turn a square without changing its name. Sort two paper colours into groups using dots and stripes as extra cues. Place up to three large blocks in a row, count each once, then spread them out and count again. Avoid small loose objects. Follow the child's interest and stop while the experience is still comfortable.
